# Introduction

On the night of May 2, 2023, heavy rain triggered flooding and landslides across Rwanda's Western Province, killing over a hundred people and destroying thousands of homes. It's the kind of disaster where fast, accurate information about where the water actually is can genuinely change how quickly help reaches the people who need it.

The problem is that the same storms that cause this kind of flooding also fill the sky with the exact cloud cover that blinds ordinary satellite cameras. Rwanda's rainy seasons and its cloudiest months are the same months — during peak rainy season, the sky can be mostly or fully overcast more than 80% of the time. An optical satellite that happens to pass overhead on the day of a flood, under that kind of cloud cover, simply can't see through it. By the time the sky clears, the water is often already gone.

Radar satellites don't have this problem — they see through cloud, rain, and darkness. Sentinel-1, a radar satellite run by the European Space Agency, is also free and open to anyone. That combination — all-weather, zero-cost — makes it an obvious candidate for flood monitoring in a country like Rwanda. And it's already been tested here: a prior study successfully used it to map flooding in the Lower Nyabarongo river valley, and the UN's satellite response team used it (alongside other imagery) to map this exact May 2023 event.

So the interesting question left isn't whether this technology can work in Rwanda. It's narrower and more practical: **what can a simple, freely available version of this method actually achieve on its own, in the steep, mixed terrain that makes up much of western Rwanda — terrain very different from the flatter floodplain where it's already been proven — and what would it take, in real, checkable terms, to close whatever gap remains?**

This brief answers that question directly. It walks through a Sentinel-1 flood detection pipeline built and run over the Nyabihu/Ngororero corridor for the May 2023 event, reports what it found and what it honestly couldn't do, and follows that technical result through to a concrete cost comparison — what free data gets an institution like Rwanda's disaster management agency (MINEMA), what commercial alternatives cost by the scene, and what free-but-overlooked pathways already exist in between. The goal isn't to prove that satellites can find floods — that's already known. It's to turn a vague sense that "better data would help" into specific numbers a real institution could actually act on.
