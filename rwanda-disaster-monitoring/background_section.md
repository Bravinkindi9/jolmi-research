# Background

## Rwanda's Terrain and Rainy Seasons

Rwanda is called the "Land of a Thousand Hills" for a reason — the whole country is steep, hilly, and cut through with narrow valleys. That terrain is part of what makes the land so fertile, but it also means rainwater doesn't spread out and soak in slowly. It funnels fast, down steep slopes, into narrow valleys, and straight into rivers — which is exactly the recipe for flash floods and landslides.

Rwanda gets most of its rain in two stretches each year: the "long rains" from March to May, and a shorter wet spell from September to December. April is typically the wettest and cloudiest month of the year, and it's also historically one of the most flood-prone. That overlap — heaviest rain and heaviest cloud cover happening at the same time — is at the center of the whole problem this study is about.

Not every part of the country floods the same way. In the lower Nyabarongo river valley, floods tend to build up slowly and stick around for days, since it's a broad, gentle floodplain. In steep western catchments like the Sebeya river, it's the opposite: a heavy storm can trigger a flood that peaks and drains away again in under a day. Those two flood types need very different monitoring — a slow flood gives you time to catch it on a later satellite pass; a flash flood might be over before the satellite even flies overhead.

## The May 2–3, 2023 Disaster

On the night of May 2 into May 3, 2023, intense rainfall triggered flooding and landslides across Rwanda's Western Province. The districts of Ngororero, Rubavu, Nyabihu, Rutsiro, and Karongi were among the hardest hit.

The exact numbers reported afterward vary depending on which report you look at and when it was published — this is common after major disasters, as early estimates get revised once fuller assessments are done. Early government reporting recorded 131 deaths. A later, broader assessment by the Rwanda Red Cross and IFRC, covering a wider window (May 1–6) and 14 districts total, reported 137 deaths, 5,472 destroyed houses, and roughly 51,900 people affected. We use the later, more thoroughly assessed figures in this study, but we're flagging the discrepancy here rather than presenting one number as if it were the only one ever reported.

This event is a strong case study for a few reasons: it's well documented by multiple independent sources (government, Red Cross, and a UN satellite response), it happened in the steep western terrain this study is specifically interested in, and it triggered enough international response that comparison data — including a formal satellite disaster response — already exists to check our own results against.

## Why Ordinary Satellites Struggle Here

Most people's mental image of a "satellite photo" is an optical satellite — a camera in space, taking pictures using sunlight, the same basic idea as a regular photo. Rwanda's Sentinel-2 and Landsat satellites work this way, and they're genuinely good at mapping water, vegetation, and land use, when the sky is clear.

The problem is exactly the timing. Rwanda's cloud cover peaks during the same months its floods peak — during the heaviest part of the rainy season, the sky can be mostly or fully overcast more than 80% of the time. An optical satellite simply can't see through that. If it happens to pass overhead on the day of a flood but the sky is covered in cloud, that image is unusable, and the next clear shot might not come for another five to sixteen days — by which point the flood has already drained away, and all you're left with is faint after-the-fact clues like disturbed soil or damaged crops, not the flood itself.

This is the exact gap that radar can close, which is what this study set out to test directly rather than take on faith.
