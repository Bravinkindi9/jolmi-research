# Discussion

## Bringing the Technical Result and the Access Question Together

Two things came out of this study, and neither means much without the other.

The first is technical: a simple, entirely free method, run over a genuinely difficult stretch of terrain, produced a plausible, visually-validated flood map — but with real, honestly-reported uncertainty (a 45–150 hectare range depending on threshold), and with a known automatic-thresholding shortcut that failed outright rather than quietly producing a wrong answer.

The second is economic: closing the specific gaps that showed up — faster revisit times, more reliable detection in mixed terrain, the ability to see flooding in villages and vegetation — has a real, checkable price tag, ranging from a few hundred dollars for a single targeted commercial scene up to a few thousand, alongside a set of free-but-underused institutional pathways (the Charter, open data archives, ESA's research access program) that aren't currently built into how Rwanda responds to floods.

Put together, the finding isn't "free data works" or "free data fails." It's more specific and more useful than either: **free data gets you a real, working baseline in exactly the conditions this study tested, but the size of the remaining gap — and what it would cost to close — is now something that can be stated in actual numbers, rather than gestured at.**

## Why This Matters Beyond One Case Study

The value of pinning that gap down in numbers isn't just academic. An agency like MINEMA or the Rwanda Water Resources Board doesn't need to be told, in the abstract, that "better data would help." They need to know, concretely: is a $675 Umbra scene a reasonable ask for a specific high-risk district ahead of the rainy season? Is triggering the International Charter worth pursuing routinely, given it requires an international intermediary and only activates after a disaster is already severe? Is applying to ESA's Third Party Missions program, despite the wait, worth doing for a university research group building local capacity? Those are decisions that need a number attached to them, not just a general sense that "commercial data is better." This study's contribution is providing a small, honestly-scoped piece of that number — grounded in a real event, a real method, and real, checked prices, rather than an estimate.

## What This Study Doesn't Settle

It's worth being direct about what this discussion can't responsibly claim. This study does not show that free data is "good enough" for operational disaster response — the double-bounce blind spot alone (missing flooded buildings and vegetation) is serious enough that no emergency manager should rely on this exact method alone for life-safety decisions. Nor does it show that commercial data is a straightforward fix — a $675–$3,250 scene solves resolution and timing, but says nothing about whether an institution has the staff, training, or workflow in place to act on it quickly. The honest conclusion sits between those two extremes: free data is a genuinely useful starting point with clearly stated limits, and commercial or institutional alternatives exist to close specific parts of that gap, at a specific, checkable cost — not a universal one.
